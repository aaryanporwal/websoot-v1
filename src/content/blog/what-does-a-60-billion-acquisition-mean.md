I started thinking about company valuations because of a number that is almost impossible to visualize:

**$60 billion.**

That is the implied value of Cursor in its announced acquisition by SpaceX.<sup>[3](#ref-3)</sup>

My first instinct was stupidly simple:

> Could SpaceX basically buy all of Zomato with that?

And that question turned out to be a surprisingly good way of understanding what a company valuation actually means.

Because the weird part is that $60 billion does not mean someone put $60 billion in a bank account and paid it to Cursor.

In this deal, the money is not really money.

It is ownership.

## First, what actually happened?

The first thing I had to correct in my own understanding was that SpaceX has not simply wired $60 billion to Cursor.

On June 16, 2026, SpaceX filed an [8-K with the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026043411/spaceexplorationtechnologi.htm) announcing a merger agreement with Anysphere, the company behind Cursor.<sup>[1](#ref-1)</sup>

The structure is pretty straightforward:

- SpaceX created a wholly owned subsidiary called X67.
- X67 merges into Cursor.
- Cursor survives as a wholly owned subsidiary of SpaceX.
- Every outstanding Cursor common and preferred share is converted into the right to receive SpaceX Class A common stock.

The SEC filing explicitly describes the transaction as having an "implied equity value" of $60 billion.

It is expected to close in Q3 2026, subject to the closing conditions and regulatory approvals.

Most importantly, this is an **all-stock transaction**.<sup>[4](#ref-4)</sup>

That distinction changes the way I think about the whole $60 billion number.

## So did SpaceX create $60 billion out of thin air?

This was probably the part that felt most suspicious to me.

Suppose SpaceX has 100 shares.

Each share represents 1 percent of the company.

Now imagine SpaceX wants to buy Cursor for $60 billion, but does not want to spend cash.

Instead, it creates new SpaceX shares and gives them to Cursor's shareholders.

Maybe there are now 110 shares instead of 100.

The original shareholders still own their shares, but each share now represents a smaller percentage of SpaceX.

That is **dilution**.

The important thing is that SpaceX did not create $60 billion of cash.

It created new ownership claims on SpaceX.

The Cursor shareholders now own part of the future SpaceX.

So the transaction is basically:

> Cursor shareholders: "We will give you Cursor."
>
> SpaceX: "We will give you a chunk of SpaceX."
>
> Cursor shareholders: "Deal."

That is why the $60 billion is better understood as a price expressed in shares rather than a suitcase containing $60 billion.

And there is another interesting detail here.

The exact amount of SpaceX stock Cursor shareholders receive is tied to SpaceX's Class A share price, using the volume-weighted average closing price over the seven trading days before closing.

So even the number of shares is calculated from the agreed framework rather than being simply "here are $60 billion worth of shares today."

## But then what does "$60 billion" actually mean?

This is where the word "valuation" becomes important.

A company's valuation is basically:

> How much does the market believe all of the ownership in this company is worth?

For a public company, this is relatively easy to see.

1. Take the number of shares.
2. Multiply it by the stock price.
3. That gives you the **market capitalization**.

For a private company, things are more awkward.

Suppose investors buy 1 percent of a startup for $600 million.

People can then say:

> The company is worth $60 billion.

But that does not mean somebody just offered $60 billion to buy every share.

Only 1 percent actually changed hands.

The $60 billion number is an extrapolation from the price of that small transaction.

An acquisition is different because the buyer is actually agreeing to take control of the whole company.

In the Cursor deal, the SEC filing is therefore much more meaningful than a normal private-market valuation.

SpaceX is actually agreeing to exchange its own shares for all outstanding Cursor shares.

So this is a real transaction price, although it is still an implied value because the payment is stock.

## And this is where the $60 billion versus Zomato comparison gets interesting

When I first thought about this, I was basically asking:

> Is $60 billion enough to buy Zomato?

Yes, roughly.

Eternal, the parent company of Zomato and Blinkit, has recently been worth on the order of a few lakh crore rupees depending on the stock price.

$60 billion is roughly Rs 5 lakh crore at current-ish exchange rates.

So we are talking about something in the neighborhood of two Eternal-sized companies, give or take market movements.

That comparison finally made the number feel real to me.

Cursor is not just "a really successful developer tool."

The agreed price is in the same universe as buying one of India's largest consumer technology companies outright.

And that is exactly why the deal looks insane at first glance.

## But there is a catch: Cursor is not making $60 billion

This was another big distinction I had to make.

Before the acquisition announcement, Cursor had reportedly reached about [$4 billion in annualized revenue](https://www.forbes.com/sites/richardnieva/2026/06/08/cursor-4-billion-annualized-revenue/).<sup>[2](#ref-2)</sup>

That is an extraordinary number for a company this young.

But annualized revenue is not profit.

It basically means:

> If the current revenue pace continued for a year, the company would generate about $4 billion in revenue.

It does not mean:

> Cursor makes $4 billion a year.

From that revenue you have to pay for things such as compute, model inference, employees, sales, infrastructure, research, support and everything else involved in running the business.

We do not have a clean public number for Cursor's net profit.

And this was something that initially bothered me.

How can somebody put a $60 billion price on a company without telling ordinary investors exactly how profitable that company is?

The answer is that the acquisition process is not the same thing as an IPO.

SpaceX's shareholders are not necessarily voting on every acquisition simply because the acquisition is large.

The board can have authority to approve corporate transactions depending on the company's governing documents, securities structure and applicable rules.

And investors do not necessarily get every piece of private-company financial information.

They get the material information required by securities law.

That is a very different standard from:

> Publish Cursor's entire accounting system so everyone can inspect it.

## But SpaceX is public now. Surely the deal has to be visible?

Yes.

This is where the SEC filing made the whole thing much more interesting.

SpaceX is now public, so the acquisition agreement did not stay hidden behind a press release.

The company filed a Form 8-K.

And the filing explicitly says the full merger agreement is included as Exhibit 10.1.

That is the document where the real meat lives.

It contains the legal machinery behind the transaction:

- representations and warranties
- conditions required before closing
- what happens to the different classes of Cursor shares
- how the consideration is calculated
- what happens to equity awards
- termination provisions
- various restrictions and obligations on both parties

This is actually one of the things I underestimated about public markets.

A company does not necessarily have to tell shareholders every internal conversation that happened in a boardroom.

But when something is materially important, there is a legal framework around what must be disclosed.

And in this case, we can literally [read the merger agreement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026043411/spaceexplorationtechnologi.htm).<sup>[1](#ref-1)</sup>

## So why would Cursor's owners accept SpaceX stock instead of cash?

Because SpaceX stock is valuable.

Imagine I own a company that someone says is worth $60 billion.

I have two choices.

**Option one:** "Give me $60 billion in cash."

**Option two:** "Give me $60 billion worth of SpaceX."

The first is obviously more liquid.

I could sell the cash tomorrow.

With the second, I am now invested in SpaceX.

That could turn out to be much better.

Or much worse.

Cursor's founders and investors are effectively betting that the future value of SpaceX shares they receive will be greater than what they could have extracted from a cash sale.

And that creates a fascinating alignment.

Cursor's founders do not necessarily walk away with a giant pile of cash.

They become major owners of the company that just bought them.

That means they participate in the upside of the combined company.

## Which brings us to the slightly uncomfortable question

Did SpaceX just use "fake money" to buy Cursor?

Not really.

But I understand why it feels that way.

Imagine your company is worth $1 trillion.

You want to buy another company worth $50 billion.

You could spend $50 billion of your cash.

Or you could issue enough new shares that the new shares are worth $50 billion.

The second option means your existing shareholders own a slightly smaller percentage of the resulting company.

But the company now owns another asset.

So the question is not:

> Did SpaceX create free money?

The real question is:

> Was Cursor worth more than the economic value of the SpaceX ownership that was given away?

That is what SpaceX shareholders are ultimately betting on.

If Cursor becomes enormously valuable inside SpaceX, issuing those shares could have been a brilliant decision.

If Cursor stagnates, SpaceX shareholders effectively overpaid and got diluted for something that did not justify the price.

## And this is where company valuation gets weird

A valuation is not a pile of money sitting somewhere.

It is an economic claim on future cash flows.

That is the mental model that finally made valuations click for me.

Suppose a company produces $1 billion in profit every year.

Maybe investors think it can grow that profit to $10 billion.

They may therefore be willing to pay far more than $1 billion for the company today.

They are not buying today's earnings.

They are buying the right to participate in tomorrow's earnings.

That is why a company can be worth $60 billion without making anything close to $60 billion.

The bet is about the future.

And in AI companies, the future assumptions can get extremely aggressive.

## Which leads to the obvious question: why is SpaceX worth so much?

This part was another surprise.

I initially thought of SpaceX as:

> the company that launches rockets.

That is increasingly an incomplete description.

Its biggest revenue engine is Starlink.

In Q2 2026, SpaceX reported roughly $7.8 billion in total revenue.

- The connectivity segment, which is primarily Starlink, generated about $4.3 billion.
- The space segment generated roughly $1 billion.

And the company is also increasingly building an AI business.

So the rocket company is becoming something much stranger:

- a launch company
- a satellite internet company
- a communications infrastructure company
- a government and defense contractor
- an AI infrastructure company

That combination matters enormously to investors.

## Starlink is the part that really surprised me

Starlink works because SpaceX effectively controls a huge portion of the stack.

- SpaceX builds the satellites.
- SpaceX launches them.
- SpaceX operates the constellation.
- And SpaceX sells connectivity to customers.

That vertical integration is extremely powerful.

Normally, a satellite internet company would have to pay someone else to launch satellites.

SpaceX is both the launch provider and the satellite operator.

And rather than putting a handful of satellites extremely far away, Starlink uses a massive constellation of satellites in low Earth orbit.

Thousands of satellites are now operating in the constellation.

The result is much lower latency than traditional geostationary satellite internet, while still providing coverage in places where terrestrial broadband infrastructure is weak or nonexistent.

And the customers are not just people in remote houses.

Starlink also serves businesses, governments, militaries, aviation and maritime users.

So the business is increasingly becoming communications infrastructure rather than simply "internet for rural people."

## But here is the part I almost got wrong

I initially thought:

> SpaceX makes so much money because everything is profitable.

That is not necessarily true.

SpaceX is spending enormous amounts of money on future businesses.

- AI infrastructure
- Launch systems
- Starship
- Data centers
- Satellite infrastructure

The company reported a net loss in Q2 2026 despite generating about $7.8 billion in revenue.

So you can have a business generating billions of dollars in revenue while simultaneously losing money.

That sounds contradictory until you remember that revenue is not profit.

A company can be making a lot of money and spending even more money trying to build something bigger.

## So is all of this a bubble?

Maybe.

And this is where I think the honest answer is more interesting than either extreme.

There is absolutely a speculative component to AI valuations.

A company generating billions in revenue can still be worth tens of billions if investors believe that revenue will grow enormously.

A company generating $4 billion in annualized revenue being valued at $60 billion means investors are effectively saying:

> We believe this business is going to become much larger and much more profitable.

That could be correct.

But it could also be wildly wrong.

The mistake is treating the valuation as proof that the future already happened.

It has not.

The $60 billion price is a bet.

A very large bet.

## The mental model I came away with

The easiest way I now understand an acquisition like this is:

Cursor is not being handed $60 billion.

Cursor's owners are being handed ownership in SpaceX that is contractually calculated to represent an implied $60 billion value for Cursor.

SpaceX does not need to have $60 billion in cash sitting around.

It can issue shares.

That dilutes existing SpaceX shareholders.

In exchange, SpaceX gets 100 percent ownership of Cursor.

Now everyone is betting that the combination will eventually be worth more than the value of the shares that SpaceX gave away.

That is the entire game.

And honestly, that makes the number even more interesting than if SpaceX had simply paid $60 billion in cash.

Because now there is another question hiding behind the acquisition:

> What does SpaceX itself think its own stock is going to be worth?

If SpaceX believes its stock is expensive, paying with stock is attractive.

If SpaceX believes its stock is cheap, paying with stock is painful.

So when I look at the Cursor deal now, I no longer see:

> SpaceX spent $60 billion.

I see:

> SpaceX decided that giving Cursor's owners a huge piece of the future SpaceX was worth more than keeping that ownership for existing shareholders.

And that is a much more interesting statement.
